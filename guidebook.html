Part one: what is safe to put in?

Most AI-at-work anxiety comes from one unanswered question: am I allowed to paste this? You do not need a policy degree to answer it. Everything you might hand to an AI tool falls into one of three zones.

Zone 1: Safe to map

Material that exposes nothing if it appears on someone else's screen.

  • Invented examples that mirror the shape of your real work
  • Public material: published reports, your own website, open documentation
  • Approved process descriptions: how the task flows, who reviews, what good looks like
Use for the first draft
Zone 2: Sanitize first

Real internal material that becomes safe once identifying detail is removed.

  • Internal examples containing names, identifiers or account numbers
  • Documents with business detail the task does not actually need
  • Anything where "who" is visible but only "what shape" matters
Remove identifiers before testing
Zone 3: Approval required

Information that should never enter a tool by default, whatever the tool promises.

  • Employer or customer confidential information
  • Health, financial and legal records
  • Personal data about identifiable people
Do not submit without explicit approval

The thirty-second test

Before pasting anything, ask three questions:

  1. Could this appear on a stranger's screen without harm? If yes, it is Zone 1. Go ahead.
  2. Would it be harmless if the names, numbers and identifiers were removed? Then it is Zone 2. Sanitize it first, then go ahead.
  3. Does it belong to someone else, or describe an identifiable person? That is Zone 3. Stop. Get written approval or use an invented stand-in.
The one rule that covers everything: bring the shape of the work, not the confidential content. Almost every workflow can be designed, tested and documented entirely with Zone 1 and Zone 2 material. The confidential data only meets the finished method inside your own approved environment.

Not sure which zone your task lands in? That is the first thing we settle on the call.

Sanitizing in practice

Sanitizing is faster than it sounds. Take one real example of the task and:

  • Replace every personal and company name with a role: "the client", "the project manager", "Supplier A".
  • Replace real figures with plausible ones. The method does not care whether the invoice is ฿48,200 or ฿50,000.
  • Delete anything the task does not need. If the workflow processes meeting actions, the salary discussion in the same notes is contamination, not input.
  • Read it once more as a stranger. If you can still tell who it is about, keep going.

Part two: the five-step method

The same method we use in paid engagements. It exists because the usual failure is a workflow nobody bounded or tested.

Step 01

Show

Walk through the task exactly as it happens now, using one real (sanitized) example from your actual week. Not the idealized version in the process manual: the real one, with the messy inbox and the step everyone skips.

Write down: the trigger that starts the task, each step in order, where the time actually goes, and who sees the result.

Step 02

Bound

Before any tool is opened, mark the boundaries. Which zone does each input fall into? What must a human always decide? What does failure look like, and who would catch it?

Write down: approved inputs, what AI may assist with, what remains a human decision, and the data boundary. This becomes the workflow brief (template below).

Step 03

Build

Create the smallest useful assisted version of the task. One prompt or template, applied to the sanitized example. Resist the urge to automate the whole job: the goal is the first working version, shaped around how you actually do the work.

Write down: the exact prompt or template used, in a place you can find again. If it lives only in a chat history, it does not exist.

Step 04

Review

Test with several approved examples, including an awkward one. Compare against work you know is good. A named person owns the output: if the review checklist has no name on it, nobody is responsible.

Write down: what the checker looks for every time, the failure you are most worried about, and what happened when you deliberately fed it a bad input.

Step 05

Document

Keep the method, not just the result. A documented workflow survives staff changes, tool changes and your own forgetting. It is also the thing a colleague, manager or auditor can actually review.

Write down: the final workflow brief, the template, the review checklist, and the next decision: what you would improve or extend, and what you decided to leave alone.

Part three: the templates

The workflow brief

One page. If it does not fit on one page, the scope is too big for a first workflow.

Workflow brief template
Task
One sentence: the recurring task this workflow assists.
Approved input
Exactly which material may enter the tool, and which zone it is in.
AI may assist
The specific assistance allowed: extract, compare, draft, group, flag.
Human decides
The decisions that always stay with a named person.
Data boundary
What is removed or excluded before anything is submitted.
Handover
What exists at the end: the workflow, the template, the checklist, the next decision.

The review checklist

Run this every time until it becomes reflex, then keep running it anyway.

  • Every factual claim in the output traces to an approved input, not to the tool's imagination.
  • Nothing invented: no invented names, decisions, numbers or commitments.
  • The data boundary held: nothing from Zone 3 went in, and Zone 2 material was sanitized.
  • A named person has read the output and approved it for its actual use.
  • The awkward case was checked: what does the method do with a bad, empty or misleading input?
  • The prompt or template used is saved where the team can find it.

The three places this breaks on your own

Do it alone and three things still go wrong. They are the three we get called in to fix.

  • You pick the wrong task to start with, and you do not find out until you have built the workflow around it.
  • Your prompt is close but under-specified, so it works on the tidy example and drifts on the real ones.
  • You never think to test the awkward input, so the method looks finished until the day it meets a bad one.
